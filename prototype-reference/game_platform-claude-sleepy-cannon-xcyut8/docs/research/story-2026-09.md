# Story in simple games: evidence and platform blocks

Research for game_platform, 2026-09-30. It answers two questions from the owner, set off by Brew & Banter (game5,
`games/brew-and-banter`: a clicker with 12 chapters, 12 characters, choices and flags, and its own dialogue engine):
**(a)** does a simple game need a story at all? **(b)** if it has one, what should the platform give it?

Source markers: **[S]** search snippet only (close to the source, not read in context) · **[3rd]** page read in full ·
**[F]** forum post or anecdote · **[R]** read in this repository. Most sites were blocked by the proxy
(sciencedirect, gamerefinery, deconstructoroffun, sensortower, medium, quanticfoundry, emshort, poki, crazygames), so
nearly all market evidence below is **[S]**. Numbers from vendors' blogs (Udonis, AppMagic, GameRefinery) are marketing
material: use them for direction, not as exact figures.

---

## 0. The answer in one screen

- **A story is a multiplier on a core loop that already works. It never saves a weak one.** The best-selling casual
  games with a story (Gardenscapes, Lily's Garden, June's Journey, Gossip Harbor) use it as a *reason* for the loop and
  as a *reward* for it. The only large player-level study found no link between reading more story and staying longer
  or paying more. In that game, the players who read *less* story stayed longer and paid more (Lee et al. 2025, [S]).
- **Words read are not what players value.** What pays off: a premise in one line, characters with a face and a
  voice, and the promise of the next reveal (a chapter, a room, a photo). Royal Match tells its story with no words at
  all and is a top grosser ([S]). Gossip Harbor, the top merge game of 2025, is praised for "thoughtfully concise"
  dialogue ([S]).
- **On the web (CrazyGames, 13+), text before the first action loses players.** Poki: "Text-based tutorials fail… If
  players need to read anything substantial, they leave"; "long intros, dialogue-heavy tutorials… struggle more on
  web" ([S]). CrazyGames' own quality list says both "limit the use of text for onboarding" and "the game story… is
  interesting where applicable… balanced and well-paced" ([R] `docs/research/crazygames-requirements-2026-09.md`).
  So: play first, story after. Keep it short and skippable, and give it as a reward.
- **Idle and clicker games are where a story fits best on the web.** Waiting leaves natural pauses. The genre has
  web-native story hits: A Dark Room reached #1 paid on the US App Store, and Universal Paperclips had 450,000 players
  in 11 days, "most to completion". Cookie Clicker tells its story through a news ticker ([S]). Clickers average about
  15 min per session on CrazyGames ([R] game-design.md), long enough for 3–6 short chapters.
- **Kids 2–5: no text.** They can't read ([R] profile `ages-2-5`). Story means a guide character with a voice and
  wordless visual micro-stories. **Kids 6–8: short spoken lines.** A median first-grader reads about 60 words per
  minute by spring (Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017, [S]), so every line needs a voice. **Kids 9–12:** light chapters work.
- **How much story:** by default, a character with barks (Level 2 below). Chapters as rewards (Level 3) only when the
  game has ≥ 20 min of meta progression. Branching (Level 4) only as *flag-coloured* lines and a few endings, as Brew &
  Banter did, unless choosing *is* the mechanic (Reigns).
- **Brew & Banter's engine is a good base** (a pure engine, conditions checked when a line is reached, chapters that
  save only when finished, triggers tied to progress, a "what unlocks the next chapter" hint). It has one real pacing
  problem. Chapter 1 (≈ 110 words on one path, ≈ 28 s of reading plus a 2.2 s title card) plays **before the first
  tap**. That runs against the web evidence above. `gp qa` did not catch it, because the game calls `gameplay(true)`
  before the dialogue.

## 1. Evidence: does narrative help retention, playtime and monetization?

### 1.1 Mobile casual: match-3, merge, hidden object (story as meta)

| Game | Story shape | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| **Gardenscapes / Homescapes** (Playrix) | Renovation meta + a cast (the butler Austin), chapters of short dialogue | After the 2016 pivot to match-3, it offered "two things that none of the incumbents had: a repair meta game and a story rich with characters". It became a top grosser in < 1 year ([S] Deconstructor of Fun / PocketGamer.biz). The story is *skippable*, and guides tell players to skip it to focus on the puzzles ([S] Gamezebo). Lifetime revenue > $3B (Gardenscapes, [S] wnhub). |
| **Lily's Garden** (Tactile) | Melodrama (romance, betrayal) in short scenes between levels | $517M lifetime, 70M downloads. "Story is the biggest reason for its success", it being "the first company that really brought storytelling at scale to a female audience" ([S] PocketGamer.biz, Udonis). |
| **June's Journey** (Wooga) | 1920s murder mystery, 350+ chapters × 5 scenes | 50% of hidden-object revenue in 2020, $500M by mid-2022. Low D1/D7 but "as time goes by, their retention rates become more and more impressive", with players aged about 48+ ([S] Udonis, Naavik). |
| **Merge Mansion** (Metacore) | Mystery (Grandma Ursula's secrets) | 49M+ downloads. Growth came from "more emphasis on narrative instead of just ad spend", with ads built on the grandma's secrets ([S] Apptamin, Reaktor). |
| **Gossip Harbor** (Microfun) | Restaurant drama and mystery (a poisoning), concise dialogue | Top merge game of 2025, $677M lifetime, ≈ $62M IAP in Oct 2025. "Dialogues are thoughtfully concise, avoiding excessive reading" ([S] Udonis, AppMagic, Mobidictum). |
| **Royal Match** (Dream Games) | King Robert restores his castle, **entirely non-verbal** | A top-grossing match-3 with a *wordless* story ([S] GameRefinery). Its sequel Royal Kingdom adds a "light fantasy story" ([S]). |
| **Candy Crush Saga** (King) | Tiffi helps a mascot per episode: 1–2 frames | A thin story. Quantic Foundry: the Candy Crush audience "scored high on Completion… and very low on almost every other motivation factor" ([S]). |

Market-level data (GameRefinery, [S]):
- "Including narrative elements in the meta layer of the game has a positive impact on revenue."
- Between 2015 and 2020, the number of new match-3 games reaching the US top-100 grossing chart *with* storytelling
  and character-driven narrative rose "exponentially". There was "a 7% growth in storytelling and dialogue elements"
  among the top 20% of casual games.
- 70 of the top 100 grossing US iOS games have meta elements. Construction and renovation meta is in 49% of top-100
  match-3 games, up from 7% six years earlier.

Player-level data, the one serious study (**Lee, Kang & Eom, *Entertainment Computing* 55, 2025**; a match-3 + RPG
game based on *The Walking Dead*, 10M+ players, 9 months, [S]):
- "Story consumption in mobile games is highly fragmented, with minimal engagement during play."
- "High story engagement does not correlate with higher retention or spending in any region". The players who read
  *less* story "exhibit higher retention and contribute more to revenue".
- "Simply integrating storytelling elements does not automatically lead to higher engagement or monetization,
  particularly when not supported by the game's core mechanics and monetization framework."
- Western players read more story than East Asian players, except in Japan.

**How the two fit together.** At market level, games *with* a story sell better. At player level, *reading* more
story does not help. The explanation that fits both: story pays off as **framing, characters, reveals and marketing**
(ads built on characters: Homescapes' "save Austin" pin-pull ads and Merge Mansion's grandma films), not as reading
volume. Chart position mixes in production value, UA budgets and ad creative. The story's causal share can't be
separated from them, so treat "story drives revenue" as plausible but not proven.

Other signals:
- **Audience matters.** Quantic Foundry (300k+ respondents): "Female gamers are more likely to be driven by Story",
  while Completion and Fantasy are the top primary motivations for women ([S]). The story-heavy casual hits (Lily's
  Garden, June's Journey, Gossip Harbor) target women 35+. The Candy Crush audience is driven by Completion rather than
  Story ([S]). For our games this means **collectible story pieces** (postcards, photos, a journal) reach Completion
  players who would never read a dialogue.
- **Interactive-story apps are shrinking.** Episode fell from $437K to $252K per week over Q2–Q3 2024, and its weekly
  actives from 508K to 147K ([S] Sensor Tower). A story as the *whole* product is a declining niche on mobile. A story
  as meta on top of a puzzle is the growing one.
- **Hybrid-casual publishers recommend it for D7+.** Supersonic: add "narrative and distinctive themes… especially when
  players are the hero", and "extra meta layers of emotional content" to lift retention beyond D7 ([S]). Hybrid-casual
  is defined as ≥ 15% D7 retention "just through core gameplay alone" ([S]). The core comes first, the story is the
  layer after.

### 1.2 Idle, incremental and narrative-first games

| Game | Story shape | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **A Dark Room** (web 2013, iOS) | A text-only story revealed as the UI expands | Inspired by Candy Box. A viral web hit, then #1 in the US App Store in 2014 ([S] Game Developer, PocketGamer.biz). |
| **Universal Paperclips** (web 2017) | A three-act science-fiction story told by changes in the interface | 450,000 players in 11 days, "most to completion" (Wired via [S]). About 8 h long. The ending was put in on purpose, to say "it's okay, you can stop playing now" ([S]). |
| **Cookie Clicker** | A news ticker, then the "Grandmapocalypse" | The story is ambient: ticker lines react to progress, and the dark arc gives "a purpose to play for after extensive gameplay" ([S]). |
| **Kittens Game** | Very few sentences, "tells a very rich story… through interaction" | Academic analysis of incremental games: narrative unfolds through "progressive disclosure" of mechanics and interface ([S] Alharthi et al.). |
| **Reigns** (Nerial) | Storylets: 750 cards, a binary swipe, 4 meters, conditions | 600k copies in the first month ($2.99), 2M by 2019, 4M+ players across the series ([S]). **The choice is the mechanic.** |
| **80 Days** (inkle) | Heavy branching text, written in ink | TIME Game of the Year 2014, IGF narrative award. Premium, with heavy text ([S]). |
| **Hades** (Supergiant) | Barks and short conversations between runs; 300k words, 21k voice lines | "A bucket of possible conversations, filtered by gameplay conditions and weighted by importance and immediacy". It avoids repeats for tens of hours and makes failure a reason to go on ([S] GDC 2021). |
| **Florence, Monument Valley** | Wordless, or nearly | Florence won BAFTA and TGA mobile game of the year. "What's important is, not what was said, but what it took to say it" (Ken Wong, [S]). Monument Valley made $14.4M in 2 years with environmental storytelling ([S]). |
| **Vampire Survivors** and its genre | No story | Defined the top web and mobile action genre of 2022–26 with no narrative at all ([S]). |

Takeaways:
- In idle games **the story travels with the numbers**. A reveal is a new button, a new building or a new ticker line.
  Players get it without a dialogue box, and it arrives exactly when progress arrives. This is the cheapest story
  format for our `kit: code` idle games, and nearly free to localize.
- An **ending** helps an incremental game (Paperclips). "You finished it" is a satisfying close and a reason to share.
- Heavy-text successes (A Dark Room, 80 Days, Reigns) are **premium and narrative-first**. For them the text *is* the
  product, and they cost months of writing. They are not a model for a 10-minute web game.

### 1.3 The web (CrazyGames and Poki, 13+)

- **The first seconds decide.** "Web players behave more like TikTok viewers than Steam users… they click through to the
  next game". "Text-based tutorials fail. Pop-up instructions fail. If players need to read anything substantial,
  they leave". "Games with long intros, dialogue-heavy tutorials, complex menus, or slow unlock systems generally
  struggle more on web" ([S] Poki blog 2026, Defold/Poki best practices).
- **CrazyGames Full Launch needs** about 10 min average play, D1 of 10–15% and **80%+ of players past the first
  minute** ([R] game-design.md). A 30 s dialogue before the first tap spends half of that first minute on reading.
- **The CrazyGames quality criteria** mention story three times: "limit the use of text for onboarding"; "the
  challenge, strategy, and game story are balanced and well-paced"; "the game story or scenarios are interesting where
  applicable"; and content should be easy to extend with "story elements" ([R] crazygames-requirements-2026-09.md).
  A rejection list in the same file names "long tutorials or text walls before the first action" ([F] Cyber Gomoku).
- **What sells as "story" on CrazyGames.** The portal's "Story" tag is led by *merge/decorate* games (Piece of Cake:
  Merge and Bake, Designville, Mansion Tale: Merge Secrets, Solitaire Home Story), not by visual novels ([S]). That is
  the mobile pattern, story as meta, moved to the web.
- **No public CrazyGames or Poki numbers** compare story and non-story games. Nothing was found, and the portal pages
  were blocked. This gap is real. Measure it ourselves (§3.8: scene start, skip and finish events in the game).

### 1.4 Kids

- **Ages 2–5 don't read.** The platform's profile already says "No text is needed to play: pictures, sound and voice
  only" and enforces voice (`E_VOICE_REQUIRED`) ([R] `packages/spec/src/profiles.ts`). The top preschool apps have
  light stories or none. In Sago Mini and Toca Boca "the scenes… are eclectic and picked at random so the child can
  make up their own tales", with "no stressful time limits or rules" ([S]). Khan Academy Kids uses a **guide
  character** (Kodi Bear) who invites, encourages and celebrates with gestures and voice, plus collectible hats and
  toys for the characters ([S]). Parents praise apps that are "calm, well narrated" ([R] forums-2026-09.md).
- **Research on story apps:** story contexts suit ages 5–6 and lift engagement. But "embedded games and puzzles that
  draw readers' attention away from the narrative can negatively affect children's vocabulary outcomes" ([S], picture-book
  app studies). Story and play should be the same thing, not take turns.
- **Ages 6–8 read slowly.** Oral reading fluency at the 50th percentile: grade 1 is 29 wcpm in winter and 60 in
  spring; grade 2 is 50 / 84 / 100 (fall / winter / spring) ([S] Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017). A 12-word line takes a
  first-grader 12–25 s. The profile's rule, "text needed to play at most at a Grade 2 reading level", is right. Voice
  should be the default for every story line.
- **Ages 9–12:** story, companions and the "hero's journey" matter to 8–10-year-olds, who favour fantasy worlds
  ([S] Frontiers in Education 2022). Short chapters with a choice are fine here.
- **Families policy risk:** kids' characters must never push purchases or ads. Bloomberg (2026) and QUT found
  manipulative design in apps rated for kids ([R] forums-2026-09.md, [S]). A story block for children needs a rule
  that no story line points to the shop or ads.

### 1.5 Adults: reading-time budget

- Adults read silently at **238 wpm for non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction**, with most between 175 and 320 (Brysbaert
  2019 meta-analysis of 190 studies, [S]). That is about **4 words per second** at best, plus taps, the typewriter
  and portrait changes.
- Brew & Banter's chapters (counted from `story.ts`, all branches included, so one path is 10–20% less):

| Chapter | Words | Reading time at 238 wpm |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The Bubbling Kettle (before the first tap) | 124 | 31 s |
| 2–11 | 80–156 | 20–39 s |
| 12 The Moonfair (finale) | 380 | 96 s |
| All 12 | ≈ 1,600 | ≈ 7 min over a ≈ 30 min story |

  Lines average 73 characters, the longest is 171, and 10 of 120 are over 120 characters. The ratio (about 7 min of
  reading in 30 min of play, with chapters 2–6 min apart) is in the healthy range. **The one problem is chapter 1
  before the first action.**

### 1.6 Conclusion: when story pays off, when it's wasted, and how much

**It pays off when:**
1. The core loop already holds players. The story *explains* the loop ("restore Wren's shop", "win the Moonfair") and
   *rewards* it (a chapter, a room, a character arriving).
2. The game has a long meta (idle, merge, decorate, upgrade shops): ≥ 20 min of progress to spread chapters over.
3. The characters do the work: faces, moods, a voice, running jokes. A character is cheap to write, shows up in
   screenshots and covers, and makes the game *its own game*. That last point matters for Google Play's Repetitive
   Content rule (AGENTS.md §2.3): own characters and a premise are the cheapest way to be distinct.
4. It is skippable and collectible. Readers read, skimmers skip, and completionists collect the pieces.

**It is wasted when:**
1. It stands between the player and the first action (web especially).
2. It is text the core doesn't need: exposition, a tutorial told as dialogue, long lore dumps.
3. It is branching nobody sees. Every branch doubles the writing and QA, while one session sees one path.
4. The audience doesn't read (ages 2–5) or reads slowly (6–8) and the story isn't voiced.
5. It is meant to rescue a weak core. The 10M-player study says it won't.

**How much, per audience** (the levels are defined in §2):

| Audience / channel | Default | Maximum that still pays | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Web 13+, action/arcade (`casual` kit, survivors-like) | L1 premise + L2 barks | L3 with ≤ 6 short chapters between runs | Never before the first run. Story as covers and characters. |
| Web 13+, idle/clicker/merge (`kit: code`) | L2 + L3 chapters as rewards | L4 *cosmetic* (flags change lines, 2–3 endings) | Brew & Banter's shape, fixed for pacing. Collectible lore for Completion players. |
| Mobile hybrid-casual (13+) | L2 + L3 | L3 + light L4, events | Story drives D7+ and ad creatives, not D1. |
| Kids 2–5 | L1 wordless + a voiced guide character | L2 voiced reactions; visual "scene fills up" rewards | No text needed, no choices in words, no branching. |
| Kids 6–8 | L2 voiced | L3 picture chapters, ≤ 3 lines each, voiced | Grade ≤ 2, ≤ 10 words per line, choices with a picture. |
| Kids 9–12 | L2 | L3 + choices (≤ 2) | Grade ≤ 4–5 and short. Never a purchase in a story line. |

## 2. Story structures that work in short-session games

The levels are ordered from cheapest to most expensive. Costs assume one AI agent or one writer; localization is at
$0.10–0.25 per word per language, agency rate ([S] Transphere, Pangea, Allcorrect).

| # | Structure | Examples | Write cost | Build cost | Risk | Loc. cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L0 | **None**: the mechanic is the story | Vampire Survivors, Candy Crush's core | 0 | 0 | none | 0 |
| L1 | **Framing premise**: one line, one picture, a goal | Angry Birds ("the pigs stole the eggs"), Royal Match (wordless), Yeet the Gnome | ≤ 30 words + cover art | nothing new: title screen, covers | none | trivial |
| L2 | **Character banter / barks**: a character reacts to events in ≤ 60 characters, never blocking | Pip's barks in Brew & Banter, Hades, Valve's L4D2 rule system, Khan Kids' Kodi | 50–200 barks ≈ 300–1,500 words | small: a bark scheduler + speech bubble | low (non-blocking); repetition gets annoying, so needs a no-repeat bag and cooldowns | low–moderate, short strings |
| L3 | **Meta narrative unlocked by progress**: chapters as rewards, 6–12 short scenes | Gardenscapes, Gossip Harbor, Brew & Banter's 12 chapters | 500–2,000 words | medium: dialogue UI, triggers, save, validator | **text walls**, a gate before play, pacing drift as the balance changes | 1.5k words ≈ $150–375 per language |
| L3e | **Episodic chapters**: a new chapter per content drop or live event | June's Journey (350+ chapters), Hogwarts Mystery ("like TV episodes… cliffhangers") | 300–800 words per episode, ongoing | L3 + content packs | a treadmill; only for a game with live ops | per episode |
| L4 | **Branching with flags, few endings** | Brew & Banter (flags colour later lines, the finale varies), Lily's Garden's small choices | L3 × 1.3–1.6 for *cosmetic* branching; × 2–3 for real structural branches | L3 + condition language + path validator | combinatorial QA; branches nobody sees; save compatibility | proportional to words |
| L4r | **Reigns-style storylets**: a deck of cards with conditions; the choice *is* the mechanic | Reigns (750 cards), Fallen London (quality-based narrative) | 200–750 cards × 20–40 words = 5–25k words | a deck engine (conditions, weights, meters) — a *mechanic*, not a scene | balance; big text volume; small market on the web | high |
| E | **Environmental storytelling**: the world changes; nothing to read | Monument Valley, Florence, renovation rooms, A Dark Room's growing UI, Kittens | art, not words | set pieces unlocked by progress (a "reveal" hook) | low; costs art time | ≈ 0 |
| C | **Collectible lore**: postcards, photos, journal pages, optional to read | Brew & Banter's postcards (as chapters), Cookie Clicker's ticker, Hades' codex | 10–40 entries × 30–80 words | a journal screen + unlock hook + badge | low: optional | moderate |

Notes per structure:
- **L1** should be on *every* game. It feeds the title, covers, store text and ad creatives at almost no cost, and it
  is the minimum for "every game is its own game".
- **L2** is the best value per word. It makes a game feel alive while the player acts, and it never takes control.
  Keep barks ≤ 60 characters, on screen for ≈ 3 s, with ≥ 5 s cooldown, from a no-repeat bag (`Bag` in `@gp/core`),
  and choose the most specific matching rule first (Valve's rule databases, [S] Ruskin GDC 2012). Brew & Banter's
  `BARKS` object (pools per event, 6 s cooldown, a chance) is a hand-made L2.
- **L3** works when a chapter is a **reward**, not a gate. Rules that came out of the evidence and Brew & Banter:
  - chapters play *between* runs (day start or end, results screen), never mid-action;
  - ≤ 8 lines and ≤ 120 words per chapter (≈ 30 s), ≤ 1 choice point;
  - a "next chapter unlocks when…" hint (Brew & Banter's `nextGoal`) turns the story into a goal;
  - a replay/journal so skippers can catch up ("Story so far");
  - a chapter title card (≤ 2 s, tappable) as the reward moment.
- **L4 cosmetic branching** (flags change individual lines, and the finale picks among 2–3 variants) gives the
  feeling of consequence at a fraction of the cost of real branches. Brew & Banter's `braveReggie` / `calmReggie` /
  `lunaJoined` lines in chapters 9 and 12 are a textbook case. Real structural branching is rarely worth it in a
  session game.
- **L4r** is a *mechanic* block request (a card deck with meters), not part of the dialogue block. File it separately
  if a brief asks for it.
- **E and C** cost almost nothing to localize, and E works for kids 2–5 and for the web's "no text first" rule.
  Kids-kit example: a sticker scene that fills up and the characters in it react with a voice line.

**Localization math for Brew & Banter:** ≈ 1,600 chapter words + ≈ 700 order and bark words ≈ 2,300 words →
≈ $230–575 per language at agency rates. Machine translation plus a review is cheaper, but the jokes need
transcreation. Text kept in TS code (as today) can't be exported for translation at all. Text in data with line ids
can (§3.1).

## 3. Proposal: platform building blocks for games with a story

### 3.0 Scope and where it goes

- **First for `kit: code`** (Brew & Banter's case): a data format, a pure engine, a UI component and a bark scheduler in
  `@gp/core`, plus story validation in `gp validate`, a `gp story` tool and QA checks in `@gp/qa`.
- **Later for the YAML kits:** a `story:` section in game.yaml for `casual` (chapters on the title and results screens)
  and `kids` (voiced, wordless picture scenes). The same format and engine are used, and the kit decides *when* scenes
  play.
- It lands as its own change under `docs/guides/add-a-block.md`. Nothing in `packages/` changes for one game
  (AGENTS.md §2.1). Brew & Banter then migrates to the block as a test of it.

### 3.1 Data format: `games/<id>/story.yaml`

Why YAML and not TS (Brew & Banter used TS): the validator can check it statically (§3.6), agents and translators edit
it without touching code, `gp story` can export its strings for translation, and the same file can later feed the YAML
kits. The build turns it into typed JSON (a Vite plugin, like game.yaml). Code games may still build scenes in code
(like Brew & Banter's `orderTalk`) with the same types.

```yaml
spec: 0
lang: en

cast:                                   # every speaker; `narrator` is built in (no name, no portrait, italic)
  pip:    { name: Pip, color: "#e0a526", voice: 660, moods: [neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised, smug, worried] }
  wren:   { name: Grandma Wren, color: "#a45fc0", voice: 520 }
  pippin: { name: Pippin, color: "#2f7a5a", voice: 260 }
moods: [neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised, smug, worried]   # default mood set for cast without their own

vars:                                   # what conditions may read
  game: [day, lifetime, rep]            # read-only, supplied by the game at each check (numbers/booleans)
  story:                                # owned and saved by the story, with defaults
    politePip: false
    trust: 0
rewards: [gold, rep]                    # what `give:` may hand out; the game applies them (validated names)
events: [unlock-golems, unlock-moonstone]   # game hooks a line may fire (`fire:`)

chapters:                               # the main line: told in order, one at a time
  - cat
  - tom
  - guild

scenes:
  cat:
    title: The Bubbling Kettle
    at: run-start                       # the moment it may play: first-action | run-start | run-end | event:<name>
    when: day >= 1
    lines:
      - narrator: The key is under the pot of dead basil. The door creaks open on cinnamon and smoke.
      - pip/smug: You're late. Wren said Tuesday. It's Thursday.
      - pip: I'm Pip. I came with the shop. Like the damp.
        choose:
          - id: shock
            text: A talking cat?!
            then:
              - pip/smug: A talking cat who knows where the gold is. Try to keep up.
          - id: polite
            text: Nice to meet you, Pip.
            do: { set: politePip }
            then:
              - pip/surprised: Manners. Huh. Wren said you'd be the nice one.
      - pip: Tap the cauldron. Every tap bottles a potion.
        cue: point-cauldron             # the game reacts (highlight, arrow); unknown cues are validation errors

  moonfair:
    title: The Moonfair
    at: run-start
    when: lifetime >= 4500000 and rep >= 55
    long: "the finale: reviewed, 380 words"   # an explained exception to the length warning
    lines:
      - narrator: Moonlight falls on the green bottle. Pip drinks.
        if: cure
      - pippin/sad: Hello, Vex.
        if: cure
      - pip/sad: Hello, Vex. It's been a while.
        if: not cure
      - vex/surprised: …Pippin? You didn't run. You were HERE.
      - label: apology                  # a jump target; `goto: apology` joins branches without copying lines
      - vex/sad: I've been very angry at a shop. For thirty years.
        choose:
          - { id: seat, text: "There's always a seat at the Kettle.", do: { set: vexFriend, give: { rep: 5 } } }
          - { id: sorry, text: "Start by saying sorry to the valley.", do: { give: { rep: 3 } } }
      - wren/happy: Coming home for tea on Sunday. Love, Gran.
        do: { set: won }
        ending: won                     # marks an ending: the validator checks every ending is reachable

side:                                   # optional scenes that play when salient; never block the main line
  tom-goat-again:
    at: run-end
    when: seen(tom) and day >= 6
    once: true
    priority: 1
    lines:
      - tom/worried: Duchess ate the church bell rope. Don't ask.

barks:                                  # non-blocking, one line in a bubble
  boil:
    who: pip
    on: boil                            # the game calls barks.say('boil')
    lines: ["Triple! Tap, tap, tap!", "It's boiling! Go go go!", "Hot hot hot!"]
  boil-first:
    who: pip
    on: boil
    once: true                          # more specific rules win: this one first, then `boil`
    lines: ["Now THAT is a boil. Keep tapping!"]
  won:
    who: pip
    on: run-start
    when: won
    lines: ["Champions of the Moonfair. Still have to tap, though."]

lore:                                   # collectibles in a journal
  postcard-1:
    title: A postcard from the sea
    icon: "📮"
    unlock: seen(postcard)
    text: Dear sprout, the sea is lovely and the seagulls are thieves…
```

Line syntax: `- <speaker>[/<mood>]: <text>` plus optional `if`, `do`, `cue`, `choose`, `goto`, `label`, `ending`,
`id`. The shorthand keeps a scene readable and close to a script. The long form `{ who, mood, text, … }` is also
accepted.

**Conditions** (`when`, `if`, `unlock`) form a small language that is parsed, never `eval`ed: identifiers (a story or
game var), numbers, `not`, `and`, `or`, `( )`, `== != < <= > >=`, `seen(scene)`, `chose(scene.choiceId)`. The
validator knows every name. This fixes Brew & Banter's single-flag `if`/`unless`, which needed both fields to express
AND and can't express OR, numbers or "seen".

**Effects** (`do`): `set: a | [a, b]`, `clear: a`, `add: { trust: 1 }`, `give: { gold: 20 }` (game rewards, applied
by the game's callback), `fire: unlock-golems` (a game event). Brew & Banter's `Effects` hard-coded `gold` and `rep`,
and flags could only be set, never cleared or counted.

**Ids.**
- Scene ids and choice ids are **save data**. Like `saveKey`, they never change after a release: the validator writes
  `story.lock.json` at `--release`, and a missing id is an error.
- Line ids are generated as `<scene>.<n>` for strings export, or set by hand with `id:` when a line must survive edits.
  Translations are keyed by line id plus a hash of the source text, so a changed English line marks its translation
  stale (the approach of Yarn Spinner's `#line:` tags, [S]).

**Triggers by game progress.** `at` names a *moment* the game offers ("the story may speak now"), and `when` names
the *condition*. The game calls `story.due(moment, ctx)` at its safe points: after the first action, at a run start
(day start), at a run end (results), or on an event. It gets back the next main chapter if due, otherwise the
highest-`priority` salient side scene (storylet selection, [S] Emily Short / Failbetter QBN), or null. This replaces
Brew & Banter's game-specific `when: { day, lifetime, rep }` and its linear `told` counter, and it allows optional side
scenes.

**Why `first-action` exists:** a scene that has to open the game (the premise) is allowed only after the player's
first core action, and only ≤ 20 words (§3.5).

### 3.2 Engine: `StoryRunner` (pure, no DOM; successor of Brew & Banter's `Conversation`)

```ts
import { loadStory, type StoryData, type StoryState } from '@gp/core';

const story = loadStory(data);                         // data = the built story.yaml (already validated)
const state: StoryState = story.state(save.story);     // mergeShape-style defaults + migration
const scene = story.due('run-start', { day, lifetime, rep });   // Scene | null
const run = story.run(scene, state, ctx);              // a Runner over a *copy* of the state

run.line;                // { id, who, mood, text, cue? } | null (done)
run.choices();           // [{ id, text }] visible now (conditions checked when the line is reached)
run.next();              // go on from a line without choices
run.choose('polite');    // by id, not index: stable for QA and analytics
run.skip();              // fast-forward to the next choice point or the end, applying skipped lines' effects
run.result;              // { scene, completed, skipped, choices: {cat: 'polite'}, effects: [...], cues: [...], ending? }

story.commit(state, run.result);   // atomic: flags, vars, seen, choices; the caller then does host.save()
```

What it keeps from Brew & Banter:
- conditions are checked *when the line is reached*, so a choice made earlier in the scene counts;
- a line whose choices are all hidden works as a plain line (the validator warns about it, §3.6);
- effects run on a copy and **commit only when the scene ends**, so an interrupted chapter replays clean and can't pay
  twice. The second-review bug #2 in Brew & Banter's REPORT is this exact case, now part of the block;
- replies inline under a choice (`then`).

What it adds:
- `goto` / `label` to join branches without copying lines (Brew & Banter's finale duplicates `pip` / `pippin` lines
  under `if` / `unless cure`);
- choice ids and a returned result instead of the throwaway `accepted` flag used by `orderTalk`;
- skip with effects kept;
- `seen` counts and `chose()`;
- endings;
- deterministic replay from a result, for tests and QA.

### 3.3 Story state (saved with the game)

```ts
interface StoryState {
  v: 1;
  seen: Record<string, number>;         // scene id → times finished (main chapters: 1)
  chose: Record<string, string>;        // "scene.choicePoint" → choice id (the last one)
  vars: Record<string, boolean | number>;
  next: number;                         // index into `chapters` (Brew & Banter's `told`)
  lore: string[];                       // unlocked lore ids, in order (the journal)
  barksSeen: string[];                  // `once` barks already said
}
```

- It lives inside the game's save (`save.story`) and is written with the game's `host.save()`. It is small: under
  2 KB for a 12-chapter story.
- It is **never written when a run starts** (Brew & Banter's lesson: the kit QA's "save survives a reload unchanged"
  check fails otherwise). It is written on `commit`.
- Progress for the portal: `story.progress(state)` returns 0–100 (main chapters seen / total). A game may pass it to
  CrazyGames' `reportGameCompletedPercentage` once `@gp/core`'s portal exposes it ([R] crazygames-requirements: the SDK
  has it).

### 3.4 UI: `mountDialogue(root, opts)` and `mountBarks(root, opts)`

```ts
const dialogue = mountDialogue(ui, {
  cast: story.cast,
  minTouchPx: host.minTouchPx,
  portrait: (who, mood, slot: HTMLElement) => face.draw(slot, who, mood),   // optional; no portrait → text only
  blip: (voice) => sound.talk(voice),      // optional talking sound, through host.audio
  say: (line) => tts(line),                // optional voice (required for kids audiences, see 3.5)
  texts: { next: 'Next', skip: 'Skip' },
  cps: 45,                                 // typewriter speed; 0 = instant; prefers-reduced-motion → instant
});
const result = await dialogue.play(run, { title: 'Chapter 1 of 12 · The Bubbling Kettle' });
story.commit(state, result); host.save(save);
```

Layout and behaviour (from Brew & Banter's `.dialog`, generalized):
- **A bottom sheet** ≤ 38% of the viewport height in landscape (800×450, 821×462), so the game stays visible. Portrait
  mode (360×800) uses the full width.
- **Name plate** in the speaker's colour, a **portrait slot** of 56–76 px, a mood, and a **chapter badge**. The
  narrator is shown italic with no plate and no portrait.
- **Text** ≥ 16 px at DPR 1 (CrazyGames legibility at 800×450, [R]), max 3 lines. The component checks for overflow
  and logs it in dev builds.
- **Typewriter with tap-to-complete:** the first tap shows the whole line and the second goes on. It pauses during ads
  and when the game is hidden (host `onPause`).
- **1–3 choices** as buttons ≥ `minTouchPx` tall, with number keys `1`–`3` on hover devices (Brew & Banter shows the
  key label). The first choice gets focus. A choice is **ignored for 250 ms after it appears**, so a double tap on
  "Next" can't pick it (Brew & Banter uses 150 ms after each step).
- **Next:** a tap anywhere on the sheet, Space or Enter. Keys are ignored when a focused button owns them (Brew &
  Banter's fix). Esc is left to the game (pause).
- **Skip:** always there, `data-gp="dialog-skip"`, and never configurable away. It jumps to the next choice (choices
  are still asked, since they are the player's) or to the end. A skipped scene still lands in the journal.
- **Title card:** optional, ≤ 2 s, dismissed with a tap.
- **Accessibility:** the text container is `aria-live="polite"` and buttons are real `<button>`s.
- **QA hooks:**
  - `data-gp="dialog"` on the sheet, with `data-gp-scene`, `data-gp-line` (line id) and `data-gp-speaker`;
  - `data-gp="dialog-next"`, `data-gp="dialog-skip"`;
  - `data-gp="dialog-choice"` with `data-gp-choice="<id>"`;
  - `data-gp="dialog-card"` for the title card;
  - `data-gp="bark"` for barks;
  - `data-gp="journal"`, `data-gp="journal-entry"`.
- **Events** for the game and dev logs: `story:scene-start`, `story:line`, `story:choice`, `story:skip`,
  `story:scene-end` (with `completed` or `skipped`). These give us the missing data (§1.3): how many players finish,
  skip or leave during a chapter.

`mountBarks(root, { anchor, cast, minTouchPx })` gives a speech bubble near an anchor element:
- ≤ 60 characters, on screen for 3–3.5 s, with no input needed and taps passing through;
- a global cooldown ≥ 5 s;
- `barks.say(event, ctx)` chooses the most specific matching rule (more conditions win), then draws from a no-repeat
  bag;
- it stays silent while a dialogue or title card is up (Brew & Banter's `barkMsg` guard).

`mountJournal(root, { story, state })` shows seen chapters (replay) and unlocked lore. It uses the upgrade shop's
"new" badge pattern (`shopBadge` and `markSeen` in `@gp/core`).

### 3.5 Pacing rules (the profile decides the numbers)

| Rule | general (web/CrazyGames) | ages-9-12 | ages-6-8 | ages-2-5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Story before the first core action | **none**, except a `first-action` scene ≤ 20 words | none | none | none |
| First scene (any path) | ≤ 60 words, ≤ 20 s including the typewriter at `cps` | ≤ 40 words | ≤ 3 lines, voiced | wordless or ≤ 2 voiced lines |
| Words per scene (any path) | warn > 120, error > 200 unless `long:` explained | warn > 80 | ≤ 4 lines | ≤ 3 voiced lines |
| Characters per line | warn > 100, error > 140 | ≤ 90 | ≤ 10 words | text optional; voice required |
| Choices per point | 1–3, ≤ 40 characters each | 1–2 | 1–2, each with a picture/emoji | pictures only, no words needed |
| Reading level (Flesch-Kincaid grade of lines) | — | warn > grade 5 | error > grade 2 (profile rule) | n/a |
| Voice | optional | optional | **every line voiced** (TTS or clip) | **required** (like `E_VOICE_REQUIRED`) |
| Blocking scenes during a run | never: only `at` moments; in-run story is barks | never | never | never |
| Skippable | always (component) | always | always | always (plus a parent can mute voice) |
| Story points to shop, purchases or ads | allowed on the results screen | **never** | **never** | **never** |
| Barks | ≤ 60 characters, ≥ 5 s apart | same | voiced | voiced, no text needed |

Numbers derive from 238 wpm adult reading (§1.5), the grade 1–2 fluency norms (§1.4), CrazyGames' 800×450 legibility
and "limit text for onboarding" (§1.3), and Brew & Banter's measured line lengths. They are starting values. The
`story:*` events (§3.4) will tell us whether to tighten them.

**Time between chapters** can't be checked statically: it depends on the balance. `gp story --pace` runs the game's
bot (Brew & Banter's `bot.ts` pattern, via `host.qaDriver.demo`), prints the minute each chapter arrives, and warns
when two chapters are < 1 min apart, when the first chapter after the premise lands after 3 min, or when the finale
comes after > 60 min.

### 3.6 Validation rules (`gp validate` reads `story.yaml` when present)

Errors (the story can't ship):
- `E_STORY_SPEAKER`: a line's speaker isn't in `cast` (and isn't `narrator`).
- `E_STORY_MOOD`: a mood not in that speaker's (or the default) mood set.
- `E_STORY_REF`: a `goto` / `label` / `seen()` / `chose()` / `chapters` entry / `unlock` points to nothing, or a
  condition reads an undeclared var.
- `E_STORY_EFFECT`: `give:` a reward not in `rewards`, `fire:` an event not in `events`, `add:` to a non-number, or
  `cue:` not declared.
- `E_STORY_DEAD_END`: the path walker finds a reachable state with no way on. Cases: a `goto` cycle with no choice or
  end in it; a line whose choices can *all* be hidden in some reachable flag state (it would silently turn into a plain
  line; add a fallback choice or make the condition total); or a scene that can't reach its end.
  - How the walker works: it enumerates choice paths with story vars threaded through them, treating game vars as
    unknown so both outcomes are explored. It is bounded; Brew & Banter's test did the same thing for picks 0, 1 and 2.
- `E_STORY_UNREACHABLE`: a scene, label, ending or lore entry whose condition can never be true (it reads a flag nobody
  sets, or needs contradictory flags).
- `E_STORY_ORDER`: main chapters whose `when` thresholds on the same game var go down (Brew & Banter's "chapters come
  in order" test).
- `E_STORY_CHOICES`: more than 3 choices at one point (2 for kids), duplicate choice texts or ids, or empty text.
- `E_STORY_IDS`: duplicate scene, choice or line ids, a bad id pattern, or (with `story.lock.json`) an id removed
  after a release.
- `E_STORY_LINE_LONG`, `E_STORY_SCENE_LONG`: over the error limits in §3.5 without a `long:` reason.
- `E_STORY_FIRST_ACTION`: a scene at `run-start` whose `when` is true on a fresh save, or a `first-action` scene over
  20 words. Brew & Banter's chapter 1 would fail this; the fix is §3.9.
- `E_STORY_VOICE`: ages-2-5 or ages-6-8 without a `say` provider or audio for story lines.
- `E_STORY_KIDS_SHOP`: a child audience and a story line or effect that points to the shop, purchases or ads (words
  like buy, coins, gems, ad, shop; `fire:` to a shop event).
- `E_STORY_EMOJI`: emoji newer than Emoji 5.0 in story text or choices (the rule that already applies to content).

Warnings (read and fix, or explain):
- `W_STORY_LINE_LONG`, `W_STORY_SCENE_LONG`, `W_STORY_READING_LEVEL` (per audience, §3.5).
- `W_STORY_FLAG_UNUSED`: a flag is set but never read (a choice that means nothing later). Fine for flavour, but say
  so.
- `W_STORY_FLAG_NEVER_SET`: a flag is read but only its default is ever used.
- `W_STORY_NO_PAYOFF`: a choice with neither `do` nor `then` (the choice changes nothing, not even a reply).
- `W_STORY_ALL_HIDDEN`: a choice point that becomes a plain line in some state (legal, as in Brew & Banter, but
  easy to do by accident).
- `W_STORY_ENDING`: `ending:` values that are unreachable or identical.
- `W_STORY_NARRATOR_WALL`: 3 or more narrator lines in a row (exposition).
- `W_STORY_PORTRAIT`: code games declare `portraits: [pip, …]`; a cast member without one gets a text-only line.
  Brew & Banter tests "every speaker has a portrait".
- `W_STORY_LANG`: `lang` doesn't match the game's language (like `W_CONTENT_LANG`).

`gp story <id>` (the tool behind the checks):
- `--stats`: words, reading time and longest line per scene and path;
- `--graph`: a Mermaid graph of scenes, choices and flags;
- `--export` / `--import`: a strings CSV or XLIFF keyed by line id and hash, for translation;
- `--play`: read the story in the terminal, choosing by number;
- `--pace`: the bot timeline from §3.5.

### 3.7 QA checks (`gp qa`, when the game uses the dialogue block)

A code game gives the QA driver an optional `story` hook: `qa.story = { play(sceneId, choicePath?), state(),
reset() }`. The browser checks:
1. **Time to first action:** from the Play click, the first core input is accepted within 5 s with no other taps.
   This catches a blocking intro even when `gameplay(true)` fired.
2. **Every scene renders:** QA plays every scene (first choice, then last choice) and screenshots each scene's first
   line and each choice point into `qa/story-<scene>-*.png`, the "eyes" for a human review. At 800×450, 821×462 and
   360×800 it checks that the text does not overflow the sheet (for the longest line of each scene) and that all
   buttons are ≥ `minTouchPx` and on screen.
3. **Controls:** tap completes the typewriter and a second tap goes on; Space and Enter work; `1`–`3` choose; a
   double click on Next does not pick a choice; Skip reaches the next choice point or the end.
4. **Save:** finish a scene and reload, and `seen`, flags and rewards are kept. Interrupt mid-scene (QA `end()`) and
   reload, and the scene replays with rewards *not* doubled (Brew & Banter bug #2). Starting a run doesn't write the
   save.
5. **Non-blocking barks:** while a bark shows, core input still works, and no bark appears over a dialogue.
6. **Ads:** during an ad the typewriter and blips stop, and the dialogue continues afterwards.
7. **Kids audiences:** every story line triggers `say` (voice), and no story screen has a button without a
   picture/icon.
8. **The report** (`qa/report.md`) gets a story section: scenes played, paths, word counts, the first-action time, and
   the pace timeline if `--pace` ran.

### 3.8 What the block measures (to close the evidence gap)

The `story:*` events can feed the dev log and, where allowed, the game's own analytics. The first numbers to collect
from Brew & Banter and the next story game:
- skip rate per scene;
- players who leave *during* a scene;
- time from Play to the first tap;
- whether players who see chapter 2 are more likely to come back on day 2.

That is the one question the literature can't answer for our games (§1.1: the 10M-player study says reading doesn't
drive retention, while the charts say having a story does).

### 3.9 Brew & Banter review: what to keep, what to fix

Keep (it becomes the block):
- the pure engine;
- conditions checked when a line is reached;
- committing a chapter only at its end;
- moods and the narrator;
- tap-to-complete typewriter and speaker blips;
- keyboard `1`–`n`, Space and Enter;
- the double-tap guard;
- `nextGoal` ("earn 12,000 more gold to unlock the next chapter");
- story locks on upgrades (already in `UpgradeShop`'s `lock`);
- the story integrity tests.

Fix (in the game, or by the block):
1. **Chapter 1 blocks the first tap for ≈ 30 s.** Suggested shape: one narrator line (≤ 20 words) → play at once →
   Pip's tutorial lines as **barks** during the first day ("Tap the cauldron", "Tap fast, it boils") → the rest of
   "The Bubbling Kettle" (the choice about Pip) at the *end* of day 1, as the first reward.
2. **No "Skip chapter".** Only per-line taps exist. Add Skip, which goes to the next choice.
3. **Text in TS code:** it can't be validated by `gp validate`, exported for translation or counted. Move it to
   `story.yaml`.
4. **Single-flag conditions:** `if` + `unless` stand in for AND, and there's no OR, numbers or `seen`. Use the
   condition language.
5. **Duplicate lines for `pip` / `pippin`** in the finale: use `goto` / `label` (or a speaker that depends on a flag).
6. **Order conversations return a result through a fake `accepted` flag:** use choice ids and `result.choices`.
7. **No QA hooks on the dialogue** (only `data-gp="brew"` etc.). QA interrupts dialogues with `end()`. Add the
   `data-gp="dialog-*"` hooks.
8. **"Is a dialogue gameplay?"** (a question in its REPORT). Recommended rule for the docs: a *scene between runs* is
   not gameplay (`gameplay(false)`); *barks* during a run are. With the premise moved after the first tap, the
   question mostly goes away.
